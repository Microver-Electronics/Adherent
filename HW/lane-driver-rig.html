01

What the rig has to do

Each lane is a CB002-24V-573mm belt module: a 24 V brushed DC gearmotor with the motor winding brought straight out on pins 1 and 2, and a feedback output on pin 3. There is no driver inside the module, so the rig supplies the H-bridge.

Both directions are required because each conveyor carries a pusher plate that has to be retracted after an unload stroke. That makes an H-bridge per lane mandatory — a simple high-side switch cannot retract the plate.

Speed is the parameter under test, so every lane gets its own independently commanded channel and its own physical speed knob.

02

System architecture

Three Motoron M3S256 controllers stack directly onto the Arduino. They use only SDA, SCL, GND and IOREF from the headers, so there is no wiring between the controller and the drivers at all — and no wiring for motor PWM, because each Motoron generates its own.

04

Commanding a lane

A lane is addressed by writing a signed speed to one channel over I²C. The range is −800 to +800: sign is direction, magnitude is speed, zero stops. Retracting the pusher plate is a negative value on the same channel — there is no direction pin to sequence and no PWM to generate.

05

Operator panel

The M5Stack 8Angle carries the whole operator interface on one I²C device: eight potentiometers, eight addressable RGB LEDs beside them, and a toggle switch with its own LED. It replaces what would otherwise be eight panel buttons, eight indicators, a GPIO expander, a LED driver and roughly thirty wires.

ControlCountAssigned to
Potentiometer8Lane 1–8 speed setpoint, and on/off at zero
RGB LED8Lane 1–8 status

Switch LED1Master state

Because the indicators are addressable RGB rather than single-colour lamps, lane state can be encoded in colour at no extra cost — for example idle dark, forward green, retracting amber, fault red. Worth defining the scheme before firmware starts so it stays consistent.

Per-lane on/off

The unit has one toggle switch, not eight — there is no switch beside each knob. Per-lane on/off comes from the knob itself: zero stops that lane, anything above zero runs it, and the RGB LED beside the knob shows which state it is in. That covers the brief's "LED and power button on every lane" in function, through one control instead of two.

Give the bottom of each knob's travel a deadband — roughly the first 3–5 % reading as a hard zero. Without it a knob left just off the stop leaves the lane commanded at a speed too low to turn the gearmotor, which draws current and buzzes without moving. Pair the deadband with the Motoron's starting-speed setting so the first speed the lane accepts is one it can actually act on.

Not covered

What the knob cannot do is act as a momentary trigger — pressing to start a single unload cycle and having it run to completion by itself. If the brief's "power button" meant that rather than on/off, it needs eight momentary buttons and a GPIO expander added back. Worth confirming which was intended before the panel is built.

Check before wiring

The 8Angle takes 5 V power but its STM32 runs at 3.3 V, so its I²C lines are natively 3.3 V. The Uno R4 bus is 5 V. Confirm the unit tolerates 5 V on SDA and SCL; if not, a bidirectional level shifter goes between them. Do not use the Arduino's Qwiic connector for this — it supplies 3.3 V, and the unit needs 5 V. Take 5 V from the Arduino's 5 V pin instead.

Lane n resolves arithmetically: board index is n ÷ 3, channel is n mod 3. No lookup table, which matters when this grows to seventy. The spare ninth channel is worth landing on a terminal — if a channel fails mid-test, moving a lane onto it is a two-wire change.

Sizing risk

The RA4M1 has no dedicated pulse-counter block, so the eight feedback channels are counted by interrupt. At the pulse rates a belt conveyor is likely to produce — tens to hundreds of hertz — this is a fraction of a percent of a 48 MHz core. Above roughly 10 kHz per channel it becomes significant. The pulse rate is supplier question 2; if it comes back high, the fallback is an ESP32 board in the same Uno form factor, which has eight hardware pulse counters. The shields and all wiring are unaffected by that swap.

08

Questions for the conveyor supplier

Seven items the datasheet does not cover. One through three change parts; four and five could change the architecture.

  • 01Stall current, and running current at the rated 6 kg load. The datasheet gives only no-load (0.2 A). This sets the Motoron current limit and confirms the 2.0 A channel rating is sufficient.
  • 02What exactly is pin 3? Output type — open collector, push-pull, hall, photo-interrupter — plus voltage level, and pulses per revolution or per mm of belt travel. The pulse rate also decides whether the controller choice holds.
  • 03Acceptable PWM frequency range on pins 1 and 2. The controller offers eight settings between 1 kHz and 80 kHz, so we can match whatever the module prefers.
  • 04Is reverse operation supported without restriction? We need to retract the pusher plate on every cycle, so reverse is not an occasional case.
  • 05Is there a built-in end-of-travel limit or home reference for the pusher plate, or do we add external limit sensors?
  • 06Mating connector part number for the 3-pin header.
  • 07Duty cycle and thermal rating for continuous operation.
Highest risk

Question 5 is the one that can move the design. Retracting the pusher plate requires knowing where it is. If pin 3 gives usable position feedback, the drawings above stand as-is. If it does not, each lane needs its own home sensor — which adds eight inputs, eight sensors, and a second connector per lane. Without either, the plate runs into the end stop every cycle.

09

Assembly notes

  • 01Set the I²C addresses before anything is stacked. Every Motoron ships on address 16. Seat them one at a time, write the new address to EEPROM, power-cycle, label the board, then move to the next. Two boards on 0x10 at once is a bus fight that looks like random lane failures.
  • 02Land all the motor and power wiring before the boards go together. Each shield has six motor terminals and a VIN pair, and the lower two boards are unreachable once the stack is assembled.
  • 03Star-ground at the PSU. Motor return and logic return meet at the supply terminal and nowhere else. Eight brushed motors sharing a ground path with the I²C bus will corrupt it, and it will look like a firmware bug.
  • 04If lanes drop out intermittently, suspect supply noise before firmware. The Motorons carry some onboard capacitance and short leads are usually fine, but brushed-motor switching on a shared rail is the classic cause of I²C corruption. An electrolytic at each shield's VIN is the first thing to try.
  • 05Configure accel, decel and starting speed per channel at boot, rather than ramping in the control loop. Gearmotors need a minimum duty to break loose; starting speed is what stops a lane from sitting still and buzzing at low knob settings.
  • 06Send a general-call stop when the master toggle goes off. One broadcast reaches all three boards in a single write, faster than iterating them.
  • 07Keep motor pairs twisted from the terminal block out to each lane, and run them separately from the feedback wiring. Pin 3 shares a cable with two PWM'd motor conductors otherwise.
  • 08Label every lane at both ends before anything is bolted down. Twenty-four conductors look identical an hour later.
10

Scaling to seventy

Seventy lanes is 24 Motoron boards. The firmware side scales cleanly — lane index still resolves to board and channel arithmetically, and nothing about the control code changes. Three things do not scale by repeating this drawing.

The stack. Shields stop being the right answer past three or four boards. The full build wants the same controllers in a flat, wired arrangement on DIN rail, which is a mechanical change, not an electrical one.

The I²C bus. Twenty-four boards on one segment is past what a single bus carries reliably — total capacitance, not addressing, is the limit. Expect to split it behind a multiplexer or move to the Motoron's UART variants.

The supply. Seventy lanes at full concurrency is roughly 1.7 kW, which is a distributed-supply problem rather than a bigger-box problem. Sizing it needs one answer nobody has given yet: how many lanes run simultaneously in the unloading routine? If lanes fire in sequence rather than together, the final supply is a fraction of that figure.
